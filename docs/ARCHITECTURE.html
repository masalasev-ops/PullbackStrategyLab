What this is, in plain terms

There is a pattern in stock prices that a number of traders claim to make money from. This project does not assume the claim is true. It builds a machine that tests it honestly, with fake money, every day, and then tries to improve the rule using evidence the machine collects about its own behaviour.

Almost everyone who attempts this makes the same mistake: they record their trades, add up the profit, and conclude something. That does not work here, and understanding why is the key to the rest of the document.

Why the trade log is the wrong place to look

This strategy loses on roughly three trades out of four. It survives because the occasional winner is worth ten or twenty times what a loss costs. So the result over any short period is decided by whether one or two big winners happened to land, not by whether the rule is any good.

In fishing terms: you want to know whether a spot on the river is good. You cast four times, catch nothing, conclude it is bad. But the fish there are rare and enormous, so four casts tells you nothing. You would need hundreds.

On this strategy's published figures it takes roughly 420 trades to establish that it makes money at all, and over 1,100 to tell whether a small improvement helped. At a few trades a day that is years, by which point the market has changed and the answer is stale.

So the lab records something else as well, and this is the central design idea. Every night it writes down every stock that matched the pattern, whether or not it will be traded, then over the following ten sessions records what each of those stocks actually did.

The trading does not stop or wait. A subset of those same setups gets a committed plan, and the ones that trigger open a position the very next session and run to their exits, days for a short and weeks for a long. What takes two weeks is the follow-up measurement, and it runs on every flagged setup in parallel with whatever the trades are doing.

A typical night makes the ratio concrete. Twenty-two stocks are flagged and all twenty-two are followed up. Fourteen pass every check and get a plan. Five trade through their trigger the next morning. Two survive the concurrency and risk caps and actually open. So the same night yields twenty-two observations and two trades, and the twenty are not a consolation prize for the trades that did not happen. A stock nobody bought is worth exactly as much to the evidence store as one that filled, which is the entire reason the store exists.

Back to the river: rather than judging the spot by your four casts, you mark every promising place along the bank, come back later, and record what was actually swimming at each one. You are measuring the judgement instead of the luck of the casting.

The two patterns

The lab tests both directions. They are mirror images with the same skeleton and different management.

The long side buy

A stock jumps sharply on unusually heavy trading. Something changed: an earnings surprise, a product, a shift affecting its industry. Moves like that often continue for weeks but never in a straight line. After the jump some holders take profit and the price drifts down for a few days. Then, if the enthusiasm is real, it turns back up. The strategy waits through the drift and buys as it ends.

The short side sell

The same thing upside down. A stock has been falling steadily, its moving averages sloping down and squeezing together. It bounces for a few days into those falling averages, which act as a ceiling rather than a floor. The strategy sells short into that bounce as it stalls, betting the decline resumes.

Selling short, for readers who have not met it

You borrow shares you do not own, sell them, and buy them back later. If the price fell in between, you keep the difference. It is the ordinary trade run backwards, and everything about the strategy inverts with it: the give-up point sits above rather than below, and the profit comes from the price falling.

Shorts are managed far more quickly than longs. Longs are allowed to run for weeks on a trailing exit. Shorts are taken in pieces and closed fast, because declines are sharper and shorter than advances, and because being wrong on a short has no natural ceiling.

What makes either one a strategy rather than a hunch

Before anything is bought or sold, two prices are written down: the entry, and the price at which you admit you were wrong. The second is deliberately very close, typically two or three percent away.

That closeness is the whole trick. Because the maximum loss is small and known in advance, you can afford to be wrong most of the time. Lose three percent three times, then make forty, and you are well ahead.

Everything is therefore measured in R, meaning "the amount you were risking". A loss is −1R. A trade that made twelve times what you risked is +12R. R lets you compare a trade in a $9 stock against one in a $400 stock.

The one thing to take away

The plan is written before the market opens and never touched afterwards. Most of this architecture exists to protect that single property, because a strategy test whose rules can move after you have seen the outcome is not a test of anything.

Collect
Downloads the whole US market's closing prices in one bulk request, plus splits. Splits matter more than they sound: if a company divides its shares four-for-one and nobody tells the system, every average it calculates becomes nonsense overnight and the nonsense looks entirely plausible.
Find
Computes each stock's averages and daily range, then runs two detectors, one per direction. Anything passing becomes a candidate for tomorrow.
Plan
Turns each candidate into a committed instruction: enter here, give up here, this many shares. Written and locked before the market opens.
Replay
Downloads the day's minute bars after the close and works out what would have happened to each plan. Prices at this resolution are published two to three hours after the close, so watching live buys nothing and costs a great deal of infrastructure.
Record
Writes everything down, including plans that never triggered and orders blocked by a limit. Then returns over ten days to record what every flagged stock actually did.
Numbers
Classifies why each loss happened, computes the theoretical ceiling on the win rate from the actual outcome distribution, and scores each variant against the baseline.
Research
Finds pairs of setups that look identical but behaved differently, summarises the evidence, asks the AI one question a week, and files the answer as a written proposal that changes nothing on its own.
Why the wall exists

An AI that can both suggest a change and put it into production will, given enough attempts, find a rule that looks brilliant on data it has already seen and is worthless on anything new. This is not an AI failing. It is what happens to any search process turned loose on a fixed dataset, including a human with a spreadsheet and a free afternoon.

IndicatorEngine
Averages computed locally from stored prices, never requested from the provider. Moving averages are recursive, so today's value needs only today's close and yesterday's stored value. Asking the provider to calculate them instead would cost about 45,000 API calls a day for arithmetic that is one loop.
ScanEngine
Six scans, three per direction: yesterday's biggest gainers and decliners, stocks that gapped away from their previous close in either direction, and the biggest movers of the past month both ways. A stock must appear on one to be eligible, because the premise is that something happened.

Each scan takes the top 50 universe names by its own magnitude, ranked 1 to 50, rather than everything clearing a threshold on the move. A rank cut can be calibrated against nightly counts with no forward return in the store; a percentage floor cannot, because "8% is too strict" is a claim about market volatility over the sample rather than about the corpus. Rank is also what makes the six comparable: a one-day move of 8% and a twenty-session move of 25% are not the same strictness and nothing says what would make them so (see: The scans select a fixed count by rank, not a threshold on the move).

The magnitudes are the one-day change, the gap from the previous close to the open, and the change over the past twenty sessions. All three are computed on the adjusted basis, the open through its own bar's adj_close / close factor. Read raw, a split turns a rise into a collapse, which is a plausible ranked list rather than an error (see: Every scan magnitude is computed on the adjusted basis). The twenty-session scans are where it bites: the vendor adjusts the history behind a split and leaves the sessions after it alone, so the one-day and gap magnitudes agree on both bases on the split date itself and only the month magnitudes span the adjustment.
RegimeLabeler
One market-mood label per day, from two scores. Index trend: how many of SPY, QQQ and IWM closed above their own 21-day average, scoring +1 for three, minus 1 for none, 0 for one or two. Breadth: the ratio of universe names passing the long ladder to those passing the short ladder, scoring +1 above 1.5, minus 1 below 0.67, 0 between. The sum gives risk-on at +2, risk-off at minus 2, mixed otherwise.

Two properties worth noting. The three-state form buffers itself, since you cannot go from risk-on to risk-off without passing through mixed. And breadth costs nothing, because it falls out of the detector funnel that already ran. The label is recorded against every setup but filters nothing in the baseline, which keeps it available as a clean experiment. Both raw scores are stored alongside it so a later proposal can use the continuous version rather than the three buckets.
SignalVectorizer
Copies every number the decision depended on into a row that is never updated. Months later the AI sees exactly what was knowable that night and nothing that arrived afterwards. This row is also what makes replay possible.
SetupCapper
Caps the night at 60 setups, split 40 long and 20 short, with unused slots on either side released to the other. Each setup costs minute bars tomorrow, which is what sets the number.

The split is deliberately not proportional. Short setups are rarest in a strong market, which is exactly when they are most interesting, and a proportional split would crowd them out of the record on those nights. Truncation is by rank. The cap applies to the shared candidate list before any variant selects, never to individual variants' picks, because capping per variant would leave the disagreements unscoreable.

Releasing a slot needs no priority order, and that is a property rather than an omission. Each side takes the lesser of its candidate count and its allocation; whatever either leaves unfilled is offered to the other, by rank within that other side. Both sides cannot be short and overflowing at the same time, so one pass is deterministic and there is no case where the order of release decides anything (see: A released cap slot goes to the side that still has candidates).

The long checks buy

Each check answers one question. The first four are cheap filters deciding whether a stock is worth recording at all. The last six are the pattern test.

tradableIs it tradable? At least $20 million changes hands daily and the price is above $5.In thinly traded stocks you cannot buy or sell at the price on screen, so any simulated result is fiction.
moves-enoughDoes it move enough? Typical daily range of 5% or more.A stock moving 1% a day cannot produce a 20% winner in a few weeks, so the arithmetic that makes this work never starts.
uptrendIs it in an uptrend? Price above the 9-day average, which is above the 21-day, which is above the 50-day.The working definition of a healthy rise, as distinct from a stock swinging around going nowhere.
thrustDid something happen? Appeared on an upward mover scan within the last ten days.The premise is that a sharp move reflects real news. No thrust, no premise.
dip-shapeIs the dip the right shape? Two to seven days of drifting, giving back no more than 40% of the jump.A shallow brief dip suggests patient holders. A deep or long one suggests they are leaving, which is a different situation wearing the same clothes.
held-floorDid it hold its floor? No daily close below the 21-day average during the dip.Dipping below intraday is fine and normal. Closing below means the stock failed its own trend test that day.
contractionHas it gone quiet? The latest day’s range is narrower than its recent average.Contracting volatility before a move is one of the oldest observations in technical trading. Practically, it also lets the give-up point sit closer.
trigger-nearIs the entry close? The trigger sits within 1.5 daily ranges of the current price.If the entry is a week of movement away the setup is not ready, and the situation will have changed by the time it gets there.
exit-tightCan the exit be tight enough? Entry to give-up distance no more than half the stock’s daily range.The check that fails most often, and the most informative one in the system. See the note below.
clusterAre its neighbours moving? Two or more stocks from the same industry flagged the same night.Recorded but not required. Grouped movement suggests an industry shift rather than one company’s news.
A gate handed nothing fails, on both lists

Every gate on both lists compares a computed quantity against a threshold, and every one of those quantities can genuinely be missing. A name short of the warm-up has no averages. A name with no scan hit has no thrust. A thrust that has not pulled back yet has no entry level and no give-up point. A gate handed nothing has not cleared its threshold, so it fails and records what was absent, which is a different fact from a threshold that was tested and missed.

What this closes is a pass, not a crash. Take a name whose thrust is the current session. Its high is the last bar, so the entry level and the give-up point are the same price, the give-up distance is zero, and zero is comfortably inside half a daily range: exit-tight, the check named below as the most informative in the system, passes on the tightest possible stop on a trade that does not exist. The research loop reads these results to find which checks carry the strategy, and a check that passes on nothing reads exactly like a check that is easy to clear (see: A gate handed an absent or degenerate quantity fails rather than passing).

Why the exit-tight check is the interesting one

SMCI's daily range was 6.8%, so the give-up point had to be within 3.4% of the entry. The obvious reference from the daily chart, the low of the dip, gives 5.6%. Too wide. The trade fails the strategy's own risk rule.

The only way to get an exit tight enough is to enter during the day at a moment when a nearby floor has just been established. That is why this is an intraday strategy, and why a test built on daily closing prices alone would quietly be testing a different and worse strategy while appearing to test this one.

The short checks sell

The mirror, with three differences that are not just sign flips.

tradable-shortableIs it tradable and shortable? Price above $5, market capitalisation above $2 billion, median dollar volume above $50 million, and at least 90 sessions since first listing.Three of those four are stricter than the long side. You cannot short what nobody will lend you, and the feed will not tell you what was borrowable that morning, so the filter stands in for the information. Recent listings are excluded outright because borrow is unreliable there regardless of size.
moves-enoughDoes it move enough? Typical daily range of 5% or more.Identical to the long side.
downtrendIs it in a downtrend? Price below the 9-day average, below the 21-day, below the 50-day.The ladder inverted.
averages-squeezingAre the averages squeezing? The gap between the 21-day and 50-day averages is narrower than its own average over the last 20 sessions.Not a mirror of anything on the long side. The trader’s stated observation is that the tighter those averages, the more reliably a bounce is rejected there.
thrustDid something happen? Appeared on a downward mover scan within the last ten days.
bounce-shapeIs the bounce the right shape? Two to seven days of rising, recovering no more than 40% of the drop.
reached-ceilingHas it reached the ceiling? Price is within half a daily range of the 21-day or 50-day average, or of the declining average price anchored to the last swing high.The level is where the bounce is expected to stall. On the long side the equivalent level is where the dip is expected to hold.

The third clause runs from 4.4, where VwapEngine computes the anchored level and anchored_vwap stores it. The anchor is the swing the flagged move ran from, at the minute the extreme traded in, and the average is over typical price weighted by shares (see: The anchored average price is anchored at the swing the thrust ran from). It runs where the store can reach the anchor and not otherwise, which is the ordinary case for as long as the store holds fewer sessions of minutes than a swing sits back: the fetch buys one session a night per flagged name and a swing sits three to twenty-seven sessions back. So each verdict records one of three clause sets, and the two that are not the full disjunction are different facts about identical-looking rows: a row written before the clause existed can never be anchored, and a row the store could not reach becomes anchorable as the store accumulates. Approximating the anchored level from daily bars would put a number that looks like the real thing inside the check that decides whether a bounce reached its ceiling, and is refused here rather than in the code alone.
no-reclaimDid it fail to reclaim? No daily close above the 50-day average during the bounce.A close above it says the downtrend may be over, which is the short-side equivalent of the held-floor check.
exit-tightCan the exit be tight enough? Entry to give-up distance no more than half the daily range, with the give-up point above the bounce high.
clusterAre its neighbours falling? Two or more from the same industry flagged short the same night.Recorded, not required.
The short side has an honesty problem the long side does not

Whether a stock could actually be borrowed that morning, and at what cost, is not in the price feed. A short that was impossible to place will be recorded by this lab as though it filled cleanly.

The design mitigates rather than solves this. The tradable-shortable check excludes the names most likely to be hard to borrow, and a flat borrow cost of 1.0% annualised is deducted per calendar day held. Both will be recorded as unmodelled assumptions on every short trade, from 4.7, which is the checkpoint that first writes a trade row; the journal that shows them to a person arrives at 4.11. Long and short results are never pooled into a single headline number. Written as a future rather than as a fact because it was written as a fact while no trade row existed, and a claim that something is recorded on every trade is a claim about a surface nobody had built.

Worth knowing how small the cost term is. At 1.0% a year a four-day hold costs about 0.011% of position value, which against a 3% stop is roughly 0.4% of one R. The rate is set several times higher than a general-collateral borrow actually costs and it still rounds to nothing. So borrow cost is not what decides the short side. Borrow availability is, and that is what the tradable-shortable check exists for.

TriggerResolver
Walks the day one minute at a time, only ever seeing minutes that had already happened at that point. The restriction is easy to state and easy to break, which is why it gets its own component rather than being a comment in someone else's code. One clock walks every name of a session at once, because the contention rule fills the earliest trigger and that is a comparison across names rather than a property of any one of them (see: One replay clock walks every name of a session at once).

Three outcomes and not two. A plan whose name traded and never reached its trigger did not fire. A plan whose session or whose name holds no stored minute was never asked, and it is recorded as unresolvable with the reason: the fetch did not run, the name was not in it, or the session was a market holiday, which is a day this lab has no way to know about in advance (see: A session is a date the store holds minutes for, and no calendar is authored here). Reading the second as the first would credit the strategy with declining to trade on exactly the nights the lab was blind.
Contention
Every plan that passed its checks is placed, and each is a complete instruction before the open. Nothing is chosen during the session. When more plans trigger than the caps allow, the earliest trigger fills and later ones are blocked with a reason, which is what resting orders do in a real account. Rank order governs which setups are recorded under the nightly cap and how the screen sorts; it does not govern fills. Two plans touched in the same minute are ordered by ticker, which is arbitrary and deterministic, where a tie left to whatever order a query returned would be a fill nobody could reproduce (see: Plans are resting orders and fills go in time order when the caps bind) (see: A tie in trigger time is broken by ticker, and never by rank)

The cost is worth knowing: a mediocre setup triggering at 9:31 consumes capacity a better one triggering at 10:15 cannot use. Reserving capacity for the best-ranked plans instead would require trusting a ranking rule that is currently an authored guess, and would leave slots unused on days the reserved names never trigger.
RiskGate
One piece of code applies the limits and blocks anything over them. It does not size. PlanBuilder sizes at 18:30 and the plan's share count is authoritative; this component may reduce that count to fit a cap or block the order outright, and it records which cap bound. Recomputing the size at trigger would leave plan_audit comparing two of this lab's own numbers rather than an intention against an outcome (see: The plan carries its own size, and RiskGate reduces or blocks it but never recomputes it).

Four of the six limits are applied here and the other two are named. The two count caps can only block, because there is no fraction of a slot; the two proportional caps reduce to fit and block only where the fit is under one share. Risk per trade is what the plan was sized from, so it is asserted rather than enforced, and a plan over its own budget stops the stage. The give-up distance cap is exit-tight at detection, so a plan that reached a trigger cleared it hours before (see: Two of the six limits are not applied at trigger, and which two is stated rather than left to the code).

If two components could open positions the limits would live in two places and would eventually disagree. That disagreement would be silent and would invalidate every comparison between variants.
PaperBroker
Deliberately pessimistic fills. Real orders do not fill at the price you wanted, and across hundreds of trades the gap matters more than beginners expect.
PositionManager
Two rule sets, one per direction. Longs trail and run; shorts trim and close. Sharing one exit routine across both would be the single easiest way to accidentally test a strategy nobody trades.

The fill model, pessimistic on purpose

  • Entry costs the whole captured spread, the wrong way, on both sides. The trigger is a traded price and a resting order entering on it crosses the book, so half a spread would price a fill at a midpoint the order did not get. The spread charged is the one captured that session rather than the one that existed at the fill, which is a stated approximation (see: Entry slippage is the whole captured spread, symmetric between the directions).
  • Exits cost the same, by the same rule. Trail exits and give-up exits alike. Pricing one end of the trade and not the other flatters every R figure by half the round trip, in the direction that manufactures edge, and the round trip is the cost this lab exists to measure against (see: Exit slippage is charged on the same terms as entry slippage).
  • An overnight jump past the give-up point is recorded as a bigger loss than planned. These are real, they are how the bad tail actually looks, and rounding them back to a neat one-unit loss would be lying to yourself in the one place it matters most. It fills at the open of the session's first regular minute bar, read from the store rather than from a vendor field, and it is not slipped on top, because the gap is the adverse move and a spread charged over it charges twice for one crossing (see: A gap through a price fills at the session's first regular minute open, and is not slipped again).
  • When one minute contains both the give-up price and a profit-taking level, the give-up is assumed first. This makes results look slightly worse than reality, which is the correct direction for an assumption you cannot resolve.
  • Short results carry a borrow cost subtracted per day held, using a fixed assumed rate recorded alongside the trade.

Figure 5. Forward returns are recorded for every flagged setup, traded or not. This separates "was the pattern worth spotting" from "was the execution any good", two different questions most trading systems hopelessly confuse.

Both halves
A stock can be a good spot and a bad trade. If the pattern works but the trades lose, the fault is execution and you fix the entry timing. If the trades win but flagged stocks behave no differently from anything else, you got lucky and the pattern is worthless. Recording only trades makes those two look identical.
Best and worst
A stock that rose 15% after first dropping 4% is a good spot with a badly placed exit. One that rose 15% smoothly is a good spot with a well placed one. The plain return cannot tell them apart. These two numbers can, and the distinction is what any sensible proposal about stop placement depends on.

Why each loss happened

One demoralising number becomes four actionable ones. Every closed loss is classified automatically from data the lab already holds, and a loss the rules cannot place is recorded as unclassified rather than forced into the nearest bucket: a taxonomy with no such category makes its own coverage unreadable, because a cause that is always assigned can never be shown to be missing one.

A 25% win rate where most losses are failed setups is a filter problem worth solving. The same 25% where most are noise stop-outs on stocks that then ran is an execution problem, and no amount of filter improvement will touch it. The system cannot tell you which without this classification.

The ceiling, computed rather than assumed

Most of the win rate is geometry, not skill

A give-up point at half the daily range sits 0.8 of one daily standard deviation away. That is very close. With no edge at all, purely random movement hits it 42% of the time in one day and 80% within ten. A coin flip with this stop wins about 20% of the time. The observed rate is 25%, so the entire measured edge of this strategy is worth about five points of win rate.

Adding drift barely moves it. Even an implausible 10% expected gain over ten days only lifts the win rate to 37%. Chasing the win rate directly is the wrong instinct, and widening the stop to raise it destroys more expectancy than it gains.

So the lab computes a reference line instead of assuming one. Given the actual distribution of ten-day outcomes among flagged setups, what win rate would a system with perfect foresight achieve, given that it still has to survive the path? For a population that ends up 15% ahead, the answer is 50%. For one ending 40% ahead, it is 80%.

The number that matters is the gap between achieved and that ceiling.

  • Achieved 25% against a ceiling of 50% means half the available room is unused and better selection has somewhere to go.
  • Achieved 25% against a ceiling of 28% means the stop is too tight for these names and the loop should be pointed at the exit rule instead.

You do not assume which. It is computable from stored rows in phase 3, before any of the research machinery is built.

Two experiment families

Suppose a change is proposed. The naive test is to run it for a few months and compare profit. That is close to useless.

The medical trial analogy

Testing two headache drugs the weak way: give drug A to a hundred people in January and drug B to a different hundred in February. If February is flu season the comparison is worthless, and even without flu you need thousands before individual variation averages out.

The strong way gives both drugs to the same people and records the difference per person. Individual variation cancels and a few hundred is plenty.

This system does the strong version. Baseline and variants see the same candidate list on the same nights against the same market. The measurement is the difference, night by night.

Deciding the answer before seeing it

At creation, two things are locked: the improvement required, and how many observations must accumulate before anyone may look. A variant that misses its target closes as refuted and stays visible in the record rather than quietly vanishing, on the research ledger from 5.5.

Defaults, overridable per proposal with a stated reason. Selection variants settle at 262 effective paired setup observations, which detects about a two-point difference in ten-day forward return at 90% power against the measured dispersion of that return. Effective rather than rows, because ten-day labels overlap and rows are worth less than their own number (see: The minimum sample is 262 effective observations, ratified at two points and 90% power). Execution variants settle at 200 paired trades, which detects about a 0.35R difference. Both numbers are honest about what they cannot see: an improvement smaller than that needs a longer sample pre-registered up front, and asking for one is a legitimate proposal.

Without this the loop becomes a machine for false discoveries. Run twenty variants and pick the best and you have picked the luckiest. With twenty genuinely worthless candidates, the chance at least one looks impressive by luck alone is about 64%.

The trap this loop will walk into if unguarded

Win rate is trivially improvable in the wrong direction. Widen the stop and fewer trades get stopped by noise, so more of them win, while the same dollar move is worth far fewer R. Going from a 2.5% to a 6% stop might take the win rate from 25% to 40% and halve the expectancy.

Three standing rules, written now before any results exist. Acceptance measures expectancy, never win rate. Win rate is reported alongside as a diagnostic so you can see which lever a variant pulled. And any variant raising win rate while lowering expectancy is rejected automatically.

Replay is not backtesting, and the difference is not a technicality

A backtest reconstructs a past nobody recorded. It has to infer which stocks were in the universe, recover prices that may have been revised since, and avoid accidentally using knowledge that arrived later. Every one of those is a way to manufacture a fake profit.

A replay re-reads rows the lab wrote forward, on the night, with only what was knowable then. The outcome column was not calculated, it was filled in by time passing. Applying a new rule is a database query over rows that already have their answers attached.

Weather-station version: every evening you note the barometer, the humidity and your forecast. Next evening you note whether it rained. A year later someone asks what would have happened if you had only forecast rain above 80% humidity. You do not need last year's weather back. You sort the cards you already wrote and count.

The holdout budget

Quarters of forward-collected evidence, non-overlapping, capped at eight. They cannot all exist at the start: evidence accumulates forward only, so the first quarter closes three months after go-live and the eighth two years after. The register is created empty and a window becomes available the day its quarter completes. One window is spent per decision between context-pack versions. A spent window is never reused for any purpose, and the register records its date range, what it was spent on, and the outcome.

The reason is that replay is free, and free tests are how you overfit. Sort the same cards forty ways and pick the best and you have fitted to those cards. The budget makes the cost explicit and finite.

Second caveat, recorded so nobody forgets it: the model has seen these tickers and dates in its training data. A proposal citing a famous move in a famous stock is not clean evidence. Prefer scoring on obscure names.

Finding missing quantitative signals

Signal means one measured quantity recorded about a setup, in other words a column in the table. Retrace depth is a signal. Daily range is a signal. Days since the thrust is a signal. The full set is called the signal library, and it is a hard ceiling on what any rule can be built from: if nothing measures whether volume dried up during the pullback, no rule can ever refer to it.

Nobody knows in advance which missing signal would improve the picks. The AI is not asked to guess one. It is asked to point at where the current library falls short, which is an easier question and one the stored rows can answer on their own.

TwinPairFinder
For each setup, finds its nearest neighbours and compares their outcomes. The metric: every signal is z-scored against its own distribution over the trailing 250 setups, then Euclidean distance is taken across the signals currently shown in the pack. A pair qualifies as a twin when the distance is under 0.5 and the ten-day outcomes differ by more than 15 percentage points.

Z-scoring is what makes the distance mean anything, since raw units are not comparable between a percentage and a bar count. Two setups with a 33% and 34% retrace, four bars each, near-identical daily range and the same market mood, one of which went +14% and the other minus 6%, are the most informative object in the whole database.
The question
Not "predict which one wins", which the model cannot do. It is "these look identical in everything I record and did opposite things, what would you want to see?" The model is generating candidates to test, not making the call.
SignalBackfiller
Computes a newly requested signal across every historical setup. Most candidates are pure functions of daily bars already stored, so the entire history can be enriched at once and becomes replayable immediately. This is why library expansion is far cheaper than it first appears.
SignalAdmissionTest
Judges the signal on its own, before any rule uses it: does adding it pull outcome-similar setups closer together? A signal earns its place by tightening those neighbourhoods, which needs no variant and no forward period. It is rejected if it correlates above 0.7 with something already present, unless stored as a residual.

Two proposal types, not one

The AI can only propose changes expressible in signals that exist. If nothing computes whether volume contracted during the pullback, no proposal can ever reference it. The signal library is a hard ceiling on the proposal space, and the model cannot lift it. So the registry accepts two kinds:

  • Rule proposals use existing signals and go straight to replay screening, then paired testing. Cheap, resolve in weeks.
  • Signal requests say the opposite: I cannot separate these two setups with what I have, compute this and I could. That is a build task, not a variant. Slower, and it is the channel that actually makes the system better over time, because it widens what every future proposal can reach.

Without the second type the loop plateaus quickly. There are only so many ways to rearrange ten checks.

You can see gaps without running anything. Adding signals has to cost something

The correction threshold in the pack scales with the number of signals screened, not the number shown. Add a signal and every other signal becomes marginally harder to claim. That is statistically correct and it creates natural resistance to adding things casually.

Separately, the library may grow to fifty signals while the pack still shows twelve conditional tables, chosen by fixed code using a rule written in advance rather than by the model browsing. Attention stays concentrated, the correction still reflects all fifty, and pack length stops being a function of vocabulary size.

The AI layer and its limits

Everything above works with no AI and would be a worthwhile project on its own. The AI is added last.

The model does not improve. Three other things do

The model is a fixed function. Nothing about it is better in December than in January. What improves is what it is given.

The evidence gets deeper. Month one the pack holds 300 resolved setups. Month twelve it holds 4,000 across several market regimes with variant results attached.

The signal library gets wider. Signals that did not exist become available to reference.

The arithmetic gets sharper. Conditional tables, corrected thresholds and the loss taxonomy are computed for the model rather than left for it to infer.

Most of the intelligence in this design sits in the pre-computation. Hand a model a well-built conditional table and a mediocre model reads the right answer off it. Hand it raw setups and the best model available guesses.

The planted null. One signal that cannot possibly matter sits in the conditional tables. It will show a spurious pattern in some deciles, because everything does. If a proposal ever cites it, that pack version fails immediately. This is a tripwire that fires before any damage is done and costs one column.

Abstention is a valid output. The proposal schema has an explicit insufficient-evidence option that counts as correct rather than as a failure. A weekly job that must produce a suggestion will produce one in weeks when there is nothing there. The best-established pack version keeps running as a control, exactly as the baseline rule does. A bad new version shows up as a divergence against a live reference rather than as a slow drift nobody notices.

What a proposal must contain

  • The one thing that changes, in the same rule language the system already evaluates, so there is no interpretation step between the suggestion and the test.
  • Which family it belongs to, selection or execution. A proposal spanning both is rejected.
  • Why it should work, in one sentence, committed before any result exists. A mechanism stated in advance is a prediction; the same sentence written afterwards is a story.
  • The evidence it rests on, as specific setup ids and figures already in the store.
  • What would refute it, and how many observations settle the question.

What the AI is never allowed to do

  • Change a rule that is currently running. An approved suggestion always creates a new version starting from zero observations.
  • Write to anything that later judges its own work. Every score, forward return and threshold test is ordinary code reading stored prices.
  • See results early. A variant's numbers are withheld until its agreed sample is reached.
  • Decide anything. Its output is a document. A person reads it and chooses.
What success looks like

Not "the system made money". Over these timescales that is dominated by whether the market was friendly, and it would say nothing about whether any of the machinery contributed.

Success is that proposals made against pack version 4 hit their pre-registered targets more often than those made against version 2. That attributes the improvement to the thing actually doing the work, which is the evidence and the arithmetic rather than the model, and it tells you whether the last round of signal building was worth the effort.

Ordering

The 54 components are listed by layer, which is the order they run in on a given night rather than the order they were designed in. ControlSampler belongs with the detect layer and ScoreboardBuilder with the measure layer; both were added after the first pass and sit at the end. The chart page sits with the screens rather than with the ingest components it draws from. Nothing depends on the ordering, because components are referred to by name.

Where data ownership is declared

Every store, its grain, and its writer for each operation are declared in SCHEMA.md and nowhere else. A conformance check asserts that file against the code in both directions: every declared writer exists, and every writer in the code is declared.

The rule being enforced is one writer per table per operation, not one writer per table. Several stores legitimately have two owners on different operations, and a rule that counts exceptions gets longer every time the design is correct.

Three separations worth asserting directly

The two detectors both write setup, on disjoint rows rather than disjoint columns, separated by direction. Assert that neither writes a row of the other’s direction.

SignalVectorizer and SignalBackfiller both write setup_signal. The vectorizer writes new rows nightly; the backfiller adds signals to old rows and may never touch a signal the vectorizer owns for that date.

VariantAdmitter and AcceptanceGate both write variant. The admitter writes the definition and the target once at creation and never again; the gate writes only status and resolution date. This is precisely the row where a future change could quietly let a result rewrite its own target.

The phase report

Every phase ends in a generated report rather than in a page somebody looks at. One command, tools/verify-phase, writes artifacts/phase-report.html for a person and artifacts/phase-report.json for a build session, from the same run. A phase does not sign off until it is green, and green includes that nothing is listed as unexamined.

Why "openable" was not enough

A page a human squints at produces an opinion, not a verdict. It cannot be automated, it gives a build session nothing to check its own work against, and after a few phases nobody remembers what the page looked like when it was right. That is the loop where things get built for months and nobody can say whether they are correct.

Why the fixture is frozen rather than live

Live data changes every night, so there is nothing to diff against and no way to tell a code change from a market change. A frozen fixture gives the same answer today and in six months, so a difference means the code moved. It also makes the report meaningful on a weekend, on a holiday, and on a machine with no API key.

Every checkpoint from the harness onward adds its own expectations to the fixture. A checkpoint that adds behaviour and no expectation has widened the unexamined set rather than the tested one, and the report says so.

Why both, and not just the screen

A page you look at produces an opinion. After three phases nobody remembers what it looked like when it was right, and a build session cannot look at it at all. That is the loop where work continues for months and nobody can say whether it is correct.

Effort and elapsed time

Roughly 35 to 45 focused coding sessions with both sides and the research machinery, so about fourteen weeks at three evenings a week. The calendar is not set by the code. Phase 3 needs three months of running before phase 5 has anything to score, and phase 5 needs a few months more before proposals settle. Expect the loop to close once around month seven. The cheapest way to find out whether any of this is worth doing

Phase 3 answers the project's own question before the trading and research layers exist, rather than after the whole build. If flagged stocks behave no differently from comparable unflagged ones, the pattern has nothing in it and everything downstream would be optimising noise. If the achieved win rate already sits at the computed ceiling, selection has no room and the loop should be pointed at execution instead. The migration test, worth applying from day one

Moving the store to another machine

The SQLite file format is architecture and operating-system independent, so the database itself is portable with no conversion. The care is entirely in how it is copied, not what it is copied to.

The failure mode this procedure exists to avoid

Copying a live database gives you a torn file. Worse, write-ahead logging means the most recent writes live in a -wal sibling file, so copying only the .db loses data silently and produces a store that opens cleanly and is missing several nights of setups.

The procedure

#StepDetail
1Stop the workerConfirm no nightly or replay job is mid-run. A partial nightly leaves setups without their signal rows.

3Write a clean single fileVACUUM INTO '/path/to/pullbackstrategylab-migrate.db'; from the SQLite CLI. This folds the write-ahead log in, drops free pages, and produces one consistent file with no siblings to forget.

One machine is authoritative, permanently

After the move, the new machine owns the store. Running the nightly job on both produces two stores that diverge from the first night and cannot be merged, because setup ids are assigned independently on each and the same id will refer to different stocks on different nights.

If both machines must run, one of them runs against a read-only copy for inspection and never executes a job that writes.

Two things to do long before you need this

Keep the live database out of any synced folder. OneDrive, Dropbox or iCloud touching an open SQLite file is a genuine corruption risk rather than a theoretical one, because the sync client will copy the file mid-write and may hold a handle while the engine tries to rename.

Run the whole procedure once in phase 1, when the store holds a week of data and losing it costs nothing. That is when you find out which paths are hardcoded and which secrets do not travel, rather than discovering it during an actual move with a year of setups at stake.

The store already carries what that decision needs: both stamps, and the lag from the older of them.

The three ways to blow it, all avoidable

  • Computing averages through the provider's API rather than locally. About 45,000 calls a day for arithmetic that is one recursive loop over data you already hold.
  • Requesting prices one ticker at a time rather than in bulk. Thirty times the cost for identical data.
  • Polling intraday prices during the session. About 78,000 calls a day for data that is only published two to three hours after the close.

The provider's screener endpoint is also deliberately unused. It cannot express a condition involving three moving averages in a specific order, it cannot express the pullback shape at all, it costs five calls per request, and using it would leave no stored history to compute follow-up returns from. The local store is not optional, because it is the measurement system.

What the money argument was actually protecting

The rule that the pack carries aggregates rather than raw rows reads like cost discipline and is not. At these rates the raw version is affordable. The real reason is that a model handed raw data will find patterns by rummaging, which is precisely what pre-registration and the planted null signal exist to prevent. Keep the rule; drop the cost justification wherever it appears.

The one-time calibration, and why it is not tuning

Five rows of the authored-parameters table are marked "phase 2 count check". They were chosen without knowing how many setups they produce, and phase 2 is the first time that can be measured. If the nightly count falls outside roughly 5 to 60 on either side, they are adjusted once, before phase 3 begins recording in earnest.

It read "four thresholds" against three marked rows until the 2.1 spec pass. The count was of thresholds rather than of rows and the two differ, because the pullback-shape row carries two numbers; nothing derived either figure, so both drifted. It is now a row count, derived from the table it describes and asserted by stated-counts, on the rule every other count in this corpus obeys.

That adjustment is calibration against a population count, not tuning against results, because no forward return exists yet at that point. It is recorded as a dated one-time event with the before and after counts. After phase 3 starts, these thresholds change only through the normal proposal route like anything else.

The count has to come from a population the scans can discriminate within, which the golden fixture is not. A scan takes the top fifty by its own magnitude, so a run over the fixture's thirty seeded names puts every one of them inside every scan on every session: thrust passes on every row, the most recent hit is always the session itself, no pullback has any bars, and every geometry check fails. The run over the fixture is a diff of the code path and its counts are nought by construction. The distribution a threshold is read from comes from the live universe, where two thousand names compete for fifty ranks.
